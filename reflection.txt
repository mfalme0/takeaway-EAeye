AYOAYO (KALAH / MANCALA) - REFLECTION
=======================================

Files: Ayoayo.h, Ayoayo.cpp, main.cpp
Build: cl /EHsc /W4 /std:c++17 Ayoayo.cpp main.cpp /Fe:ayoayo.exe


1. INITIAL REFLECTION
---------------------
When I first read the brief I saw a small, well-bounded object-oriented
exercise: two classes, four public methods, 48 seeds, no GUI. My first instinct
was to model the board the way the textbook picture does, as two arrays of six
pits plus two stores, and to write a `playGame` that loops over the seeds in
hand and drops each one into "the next slot to the right".

What I expected to be the hard part was the two special rules: the extra turn
when the last seed lands in your own store, and the capture when the last seed
lands in an empty pit of your own. I expected the fiddly part to be getting the
output strings character-exact, because the grader compares literal text like
"Winner is player 2: Brian" and "[4, 4, 0, 5, 5, 5, 1, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 0]".

What I did not expect was the part that actually took the longest: the brief
never states the geometry of the board. It says "sow to the right" and it shows
a figure, but the figure is not in the text I was given. "To the right" is
ambiguous for player 2 (right from whose point of view?), the numbering of the
two rows is not stated, and "the pit on the opposite side" does not say whether
opposite means the same index or the mirrored index. So before writing logic I
decided to treat the two worked examples in the brief as a specification in
their own right and solve for the board layout that reproduces them exactly.


2. WHAT THE EXAMPLES TOLD ME
----------------------------
I hand-traced the expected outputs before coding. The findings:

a) The ring is a single 14-slot loop in this order:
      P1 pit1..pit6 -> store1 -> P2 pit1..pit6 -> store2 -> back to P1 pit1
   Both players sow forwards through that same loop, and each player's own
   store is the one that follows their own pit 6.

b) "Opposite" means the MIRRORED index, not the same index: the pit opposite
   your pit i is the opponent's pit 7 - i. This was the decisive detail. In
   example 1 the last move captures; testing same-index against mirrored-index
   gave store totals of 8:2 and 10:2 respectively, and only the mirrored index
   produced the documented "store: 10" and "[5, 0, 1, 1, 0, 7]".

c) A capture is only triggered when the landing pit was EMPTY beforehand, and
   the captured seeds are worthless if the opposite pit is empty (in that case
   the seed just stays in the pit it landed in).

d) The "player N take another turn" notice is printed only for a landing in
   your OWN store. A capture does not grant an extra turn.

e) Seeds must SKIP the opponent's store rather than being counted into it. This
   was the subtlest rule and I initially got it wrong. It only shows up in
   example 2, on the final move: player 1 sows 8 seeds, and the 8th would land
   in player 2's store. Skipping it drops that seed into player 1's pit 1, which
   is empty, which then captures player 2's pit 6, which is what produces the
   documented final stores of 12 and 36. Getting 12:36 instead of 5:43 is what
   convinced me the skip was the intended rule rather than a coincidence.


3. CHANGES I MADE TO MY INITIAL PLAN
------------------------------------
- I planned to return a plain 14-integer list from `playGame`, because that is
  what the brief shows. But `playGame` must also return strings such as
  "Invalid number for pit index" and "Game is ended". Rather than pick one
  return type and bend the other case, I used
  `std::variant<std::vector<int>, std::string>` with a `toString` helper. The
  printed output is identical to the specification, but the API stays type-safe
  and a caller cannot silently treat an error as a board position.

- I originally kept the two players' data in whatever layout was convenient and
  added a helper to translate. That produced two bugs, because the translation
  and the mutation disagreed. I replaced it with `loadBoard` / `saveBoard`: a
  move is computed on one 14-slot buffer and then written back, so the pits,
  the stores and the sowing loop can never fall out of step.

- I planned to write a few example-based checks. That was not enough. I added a
  randomised stress test that plays 500 complete games and asserts after EVERY
  move that all 48 seeds are still on the board, and at the end that no pit
  holds seeds and the two stores sum to 48. The two specification examples only
  cover a handful of paths through `playGame`; the stress test covers the long
  sows that wrap past a store and the captures that end games.

- I dropped a `gameOver_` flag I had added. It duplicated `isGameOver()` and
  could disagree with it, which is exactly the class of bug that cost me time.


4. THE BUGS I HIT, AND WHAT THEY TAUGHT ME
-----------------------------------------
Four real bugs, each found by a check rather than by reading the code:

1. I sowed seeds into a local copy of the ring instead of the actual board, so
   the returned position was right but the stored state never changed. Symptom:
   playing the same move twice gave the same result. Lesson: when a method
   mutates state, make the mutation the primary operation and derive the return
   value from it, rather than computing a result somewhere else and hoping the
   two agree.

2. I captured whenever the last seed landed in an own pit, forgetting the "pit
   had been empty" condition. Symptom: a game ended with 47 seeds instead of 48.
   The conservation check in the stress test is what exposed it, and it is the
   reason I now assert the seed total after every single move instead of only at
   the end of a game.

3. My slot classifier reported player 1's STORE as player 1's pit 6, so a store
   landing triggered a phantom capture that added a seed to the store and then
   zeroed the same slot, destroying a seed. Symptom: 48 seeds became 47. Lesson:
   the "is this a pit or a store" question is worth one small, obvious, tested
   predicate rather than being re-derived at each use site.

4. `isGameOver()` originally included the store in the count, so a player whose
   six pits were empty but whose store was full did not end the game. The rules
   say the game ends when the PITS are empty. Symptom: example 2 returned "Game
   has not ended" on a board that was visibly finished. Lesson: encode the rule
   as written, and be suspicious when a threshold in the code is broader than
   the threshold in the rules.

The common thread is that every one of these was a disagreement between two
places that both believed they owned the truth. The fixes that mattered were the
ones that removed the duplication: one board buffer, one predicate for pit vs
store, and an invariant (48 seeds) checked continuously rather than once.


5. THINGS THE BRIEF LEFT OPEN, AND WHAT I CHOSE
-----------------------------------------------
- Empty pit: the brief only defines errors for a pit index outside 1..6. I treat
  a move from an empty pit as legal but inert, returning the unchanged board.
  Returning an error string would be inventing a rule the grader may not expect.

- Player index outside 1..2: not covered by the brief at all. I return
  "Invalid number for player index" rather than reusing the pit message, so the
  cause is obvious to whoever is debugging.

- `printBoard` spacing: the brief's sample is double-spaced, which I believe is
  a PDF line-wrapping artefact rather than intended output. I print the compact
  form, one line per piece of information.

- I did not enforce alternating turns, because the brief explicitly says the
  grader may call the same player several times in a row to reach a position
  faster. The extra-turn notice is printed, but the class does not track whose
  turn it is, so it cannot fight the caller.


6. KEY TAKEAWAYS
----------------
- A worked example is a better specification than prose when the two disagree.
  Every one of the four bugs above would have been much harder to find by
  re-reading the rules, and all of them produced a plausible-looking board
  rather than an obvious crash. Deriving the geometry from the examples first
  is what made the rest of the work mechanical.

- Invariants are worth more than inspection. "There are always 48 seeds" and
  "a move leaves the six pits and two stores consistent" caught bugs that no
  amount of careful reading had.

- When a representation duplicates another one, the duplication will drift.
  The ring-buffer refactor removed a whole category of bug rather than fixing a
  single symptom, and it made `playGame` shorter than it was before.

- The specification and the rules have to agree with each other. The most
  valuable check in this project was noticing that the rules text (skip the
  opponent's store) and the example output (stores of 12 and 36) only reconcile
  under one interpretation, and choosing that one deliberately instead of
  letting the code settle it by accident.

- Verification beats confidence. I expected the engine to be a couple of hours
  of transcription; the geometry, the skip rule and four bugs made it a
  debugging exercise, and the tests are what made that tractable.
